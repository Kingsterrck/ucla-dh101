Data Critique

What the Data Can & Cannot Say

The dataset we chose is the COVID-19 Data Repository created by the Center for Systems Science and Engineering (CSSE) at Johns Hopkins University. This dataset was used to support the Johns Hopkins COVID-19 Visual Dashboard, which became one of the most widely used public tools for tracking the pandemic. This repository includes confirmed COVID-19 cases and deaths all over the world, organized by country, region, state, and geographic location. Some files are time-series data, while others are daily reports. The dataset also includes latitude and longitude information which could be used to create maps showing the spread of COVID across space and time.

The data was generated by collecting and combining information from many different sources, including international health organizations, national governments, local health departments, and public tracking projects. Some major sources listed by Johns Hopkins include WHO, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, the U.s. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Worldometer, and many state and county health agencies. It is not collected by John Hopkins itself. But John Hopkins combined lots of small data collection together, which means that the dataset wasn’t produced from one unified system.

This dataset can explain several important aspects of the COVID-19 pandemic. First, it can show broad global patterns: where reported cases and deaths increased, when different countries experienced major waves, and how the pandemic moved across regions over time. From this, we might be able to indicate how the COVID spread and how we can prevent it. Second, because the data includes geographic information, it can be used to create maps and visualizations that make the pandemic easier to understand. Third, it helps users compare countries and regions, at least at the level of reported cases and deaths. In this sense, the dataset is powerful because it turns a massive global crisis into something visible, organized, and analyzable.

However, the dataset also has important limits. It cannot fully reveal the real number of infections, because confirmed cases depend heavily on testing availability, reporting capacity, and government transparency. A country with fewer tests may appear to have fewer cases, even if the actual number of infections is high. Death counts also have limitations because countries may define COVID-19 deaths differently, and some deaths may never be officially recorded. The dataset also cannot show personal experiences, such as fear, grief, unemployment, isolation, hospital overcrowding, or the unequal social effects of the pandemic. It tells us how many cases and deaths were reported, but not how people experienced the crisis.

The spreadsheet also leaves out many kinds of information. It does not include detailed demographic categories for all places, such as race, class, occupation, disability, or immigration status. It does not fully show access to healthcare, the quality of testing systems, or the political reasons why some governments may underreport cases or deaths. It also does not include the voices of patients, families, doctors, nurses, or communities affected by lockdowns and illness.

The ontology of this dataset is important. It defines the pandemic mainly through countable categories: cases, deaths, dates, and locations. This structure is useful, but it also has ideological effects. It makes COVID-19 appear as a statistical and geographic event, something that can be represented through numbers and maps. This can create a sense of objectivity, but the data is not neutral. It depends on institutions, reporting practices, and political decisions. If this dataset were my only source, I might understand the pandemic as a pattern of numbers rather than as a deeply unequal human crisis. Therefore, the Johns Hopkins dataset is valuable, but it should be read critically and used alongside other sources, including local reports, oral histories, medical accounts, and social analysis.
